6.2 Data Weighting and Likelihood Conflict in Integrated & Multispecies Assessments (Francis 2011; Maunder & Punt 2013; Thorson et al. 2017)

When fitting an integrated single-species or multispecies model simultaneously to abundance indices (\(I_{f,y}\)), catch biomass (\(Y_{f,y}\)), length/age compositions (\(\mathbf{P}_{f,y}\)), and stomach content compositions (\(\mathbf{W}_{A,L,y,q}\)), the most critical statistical hazard is likelihood conflict and compositional domination (Francis 2011; Maunder and Punt 2013; Thorson et al. 2017).

6.2.1 Why Unadjusted Composition Likelihoods Destroy Abundance Fits (“Francis Principle 1”)

Suppose a survey measures \(10,000\) fish lengths across \(50\) length bins each year, while also providing \(1\) annual biomass index point \(I_y\) per year. If the nominal number of fish measured (\(N_y = 10,000\)) is plugged into a Multinomial likelihood \(\sum_y N_y \sum_l P_{l,y} \ln \hat{P}_{l,y}\), the composition likelihood will have \(1000\times\) larger gradient and curvature than the lognormal survey index likelihood \(\frac{1}{2\sigma_I^2}(\ln I_y - \ln \hat{I}_y)^2\). Because fish school by size and because any process model has slight misspecifications in selectivity or growth, positive correlations among bins within a year mean the true effective sample size \(N_{\text{eff}, y}\) is typically \(20\text{--}200\) (closer to the number of trawl hauls, not the number of individual fish!). If \(N_y\) is not downweighted, the optimizer will distort recruitment \(R_y\), fishing mortality \(F_y\), and biomass trends \(\hat{B}_y\) just to chase noise in the length compositions, violating Francis’s First Principle (Francis 2011, 1125): > “Do not let other data stop the model from fitting abundance data well.”

6.2.2 Three Standard Data-Weighting Algorithms for Compositional Data

Let \(O_{l,y}\) and \(E_{l,y}\) denote the observed and model-expected proportions in length (or age) bin \(l \in \{1, \dots, n_L\}\) with midpoint \(x_l\) in year \(y \in \{1, \dots, n_Y\}\), with initial nominal sample size \(N_{y}\).

  1. McAllister & Ianelli (1997) Harmonic Mean Weighting: Estimates the effective sample size \(\hat{N}_{y} = \frac{\sum_{l} E_{l,y}(1 - E_{l,y})}{\sum_{l} (O_{l,y} - E_{l,y})^2}\) for each year and sets the dataset multiplier \(w_{\text{MI}}\) using the harmonic mean across years (used in MSCAA (Curti et al. 2013) and Rceattle (Adams et al. 2022); see McAllister and Ianelli (1997)).

    • Critique (Francis 2011, 1128–31): Assumes residuals across bins \(l\) within the same year \(y\) are uncorrelated. When residuals are positively correlated across adjacent length bins (the norm when growth or selectivity varies), McAllister–Ianelli dramatically overweights the composition data!

  2. Francis (2011) Method TA1.8 (Mean-Length / Mean-Age Residual Variance): Collapses each year’s length composition into its observed mean length \(\bar{O}_y = \sum_l x_l O_{l,y}\) and expected mean length \(\bar{E}_y = \sum_l x_l E_{l,y}\), with multinomial within-year variance \(v_y = \sum_l x_l^2 E_{l,y} - \bar{E}_y^2\). Because collapsing to the annual mean \(\bar{O}_y\) absorbs all within-year inter-bin correlations, the standardized mean-length residuals \(\frac{\bar{O}_y - \bar{E}_y}{\sqrt{v_y / (w N_y)}}\) must have unit variance, yielding the Francis TA1.8 weight multiplier (Francis 2011, Appendix A, Table A1):

    \[w_{\text{Francis}} = \left[ \text{Var}_{y}\!\left( \frac{\bar{O}_y - \bar{E}_y}{\sqrt{v_y / N_y}} \right) \right]^{-1}\]

  3. Self-Weighting Likelihoods (Dirichlet-Multinomial / Logistic-Normal / SMS Dirichlet): Rather than outer fixed-point reweighting loops (Francis TA1.8 or Gadget two-stage reweighting), modern TMB and SS3 models (SMS, EcoState, LIME (Rudd and Thorson 2018), SS3 (Thorson et al. 2017)) use a Dirichlet or Dirichlet-Multinomial likelihood with an estimable dispersion parameter \(\theta\), where effective sample size is either linear in \(N_y\): \[N_{\text{eff}, y} = \frac{1 + \theta N_y}{1 + \theta}\] or saturating at \(1 + \beta\): \[N_{\text{eff}, y} = \frac{N_y + N_y \beta}{N_y + \beta}\] In a 4,500-replicate simulation study and Pacific hake application, Thorson et al. (2017) proved that simultaneous DM estimation eliminates the severe bias of unweighted multinomial likelihoods, matches the accuracy and precision of iterative McAllister–Ianelli tuning for \(M\), \(\ln R_0\), and spawning output, and propagates data-weighting uncertainty directly into the joint Hessian and MCMC posterior!

6.3 Parameter Estimation and Uncertainty Quantification Engines

6.3.1 Frequentist / Empirical Bayes State-Space Estimation in TMB (Kristensen et al. 2016)

Modern statistical stock assessment models (SAM (Nielsen and Berg 2014), smsR, Rceattle, gadget3, EcoState (Thorson et al. 2024)) are built on Template Model Builder (TMB) (Kristensen et al. 2016) or its pure-R interface RTMB.

Let \(\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^p\) denote the vector of fixed effects (life-history scaling parameters, selectivities, catchabilities, suitability parameters \(\beta_i, \sigma_i\), and variance parameters \(\sigma_{\text{proc}}, \sigma_{\text{obs}}\)) and let \(\mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{R}^m\) denote the vector of latent random effects (process errors) (e.g., annual recruitment deviations \(\epsilon_{R,i,y}\), time-varying fishing mortality random walks \(\ln F_{f,y}\), or biomass process errors \(\epsilon_{B,i,y}\)). Given joint negative log-likelihood \(f(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \mathbf{u}) = -\ln p(\mathbf{y}, \mathbf{u} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta})\), TMB approximates the marginal likelihood \(L(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \int \exp(-f(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \mathbf{u})) \, d\mathbf{u}\) via the Laplace Approximation (Kristensen et al. 2016, Eq. 2):

\[-\ln L^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = f\!\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \hat{\mathbf{u}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right) + \frac{1}{2} \ln \det \mathbf{H}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) - \frac{m}{2} \ln(2\pi)\]

where: - \(\hat{\mathbf{u}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \arg\min_{\mathbf{u}} f(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \mathbf{u})\) is the inner-loop empirical Bayes mode of the random effects, and - \(\mathbf{H}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \left.\frac{\partial^2 f(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \mathbf{u})}{\partial \mathbf{u} \partial \mathbf{u}^\top}\right|_{\mathbf{u} = \hat{\mathbf{u}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\) is the sparse \(m \times m\) Hessian matrix of the random effects, factored via sparse Cholesky decomposition (CHOLMOD).

Using up to third-order automatic differentiation (CppAD), TMB computes the exact gradient \(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} (-\ln L^{*}(\boldsymbol{\theta}))\) of the Laplace-approximated marginal likelihood in \(O(1)\) reverse-mode sweeps, allowing nlminb or optim to estimate hundreds of fixed effects and thousands of random effects in seconds to minutes, followed by joint precision matrix inversion (sdreport) for derived quantities (\(\text{SSB}_{i,y}, M2_{i,a,y}, F_{\text{MSY}, i}\)).

6.3.2 Bayesian, Evolutionary, and Ensemble Approaches (Zhang et al. 2015, 2016; Spence et al. 2016, 2018, 2021)

When models are implemented as black-box simulation packages without automatic differentiation (or when likelihood surfaces are rugged and multimodal), researchers have developed four complementary calibration and uncertainty frameworks for mizer and LeMaRns:

  1. Two-Step Genetic Algorithm + Parallel Tempering MCMC (Zhang et al. 2015, 2016): In calibrating a multispecies mizer model to data-poor and data-rich ecosystems (Zhang et al. 2015, 2016), Zhang et al. found that standard gradient descent or single-chain Metropolis–Hastings gets trapped in local modes where one or more species go extinct during spin-up. They introduced a two-stage global optimizer:
    • Stage 1: A parallel Real-Coded Genetic Algorithm (GA) searches the high-dimensional parameter space \((\ln R_{\max, i}, \kappa, q_{f,i}, F_i)\) using a survival penalty for extinct species to locate the global basin of attraction.
    • Stage 2: Parallel Tempering MCMC (running multiple interacting Markov chains at temperatures \(T_1 = 1 < T_2 < \dots < T_M\)) samples the posterior distribution across multimodal ridges.
  2. History Matching + Sequential Monte Carlo (Spence et al. 2016, 2021): To calibrate mizer (Spence et al. 2016) and LeMaRns (Spence, Thorpe, et al. 2021) to North Sea time series while explicitly accounting for structural model discrepancy \(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\text{disc}}\) (the difference between the true ocean and the best possible parameterization of the model), Spence et al. combined:
    • History Matching: Defines an implausibility measure \(I_k(\boldsymbol{\theta})\) for summary statistic \(k\): \[I_k(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{\left|y^{\text{obs}}_k - \mathbb{E}[f_k(\boldsymbol{\theta})]\right|}{\sqrt{\text{Var}(y^{\text{obs}}_k) + \text{Var}(f_k(\boldsymbol{\theta})) + \text{Var}(\eta_{\text{disc}, k})}}\] and rules out any parameter vector \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) where \(\max_k I_k(\boldsymbol{\theta}) > 3\) (by Pukelsheim’s \(3\sigma\) rule), rapidly shrinking the feasible space before running full time-series likelihood evaluations.
    • Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC): Propagates a particle cloud \(\left\{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(p)}\right\}_{p=1}^P\) through sequential annealing or time-series filtering steps using Normalizing Flows / Metropolis-Hastings rejuvenation moves.
  3. Bayesian Multi-Model Ensembles (Spence et al. 2018): Instead of selecting a single “winner” model via AIC, Spence et al. (2018) constructed a hierarchical Bayesian ensemble linking five distinct multispecies simulators (EcoPath with Ecosim, LeMans, mizer, SMS, and single-species assessments) as biased noisy realizations around a latent true ecosystem trajectory \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_t\), decomposing structural discrepancy into shared vs. simulator-specific discrepancy processes.

6.4 Architectural Blueprint for Next-Generation Statistical Dynamic Stock Assessment in mizer

In their 10-year review of mizer, Pennino et al. (2025, Section “Challenges and Future Directions”, p. 9) identify the central barrier preventing mizer from being used as a routine tactical stock assessment tool: > “First, there is a lack of a standardized optimization routine for parameter estimation and uncertainty quantification… Second, mizer models are typically calibrated to a time-averaged steady state rather than fitted dynamically to time series of catches, survey indices, and size compositions.”

Drawing together the mathematical lessons from SMS (Chapter 2), Gadget/gadget3, MULTIFAN-CL/SS3, and Punt et al. (2016) (Chapter 3), EcoState (Chapter 4), and stomach content likelihoods (Chapter 5), we can synthesize a concrete, five-pillar architectural roadmap for transforming mizer into a differentiable, state-space statistical multispecies stock assessment model (mizer-TMB / mizer-RTMB).

6.4.1 Pillar 1: Resolving Cohort Dispersion via Jump-Growth Diffusion (Datta, Delius & Law 2010)

When mizer is projected dynamically with interannual recruitment fluctuations \(R_{\text{dd}, i}(t) \exp(\epsilon_{R,i,y})\), a strong year class enters at \(w_0\) and propagates across the weight grid. - Problem: Under the standard first-order upwind scheme, cohort width is governed by numerical grid viscosity \(D_{\text{num}} \approx \frac{1}{2} g_i \Delta w\), which fails to match the true biological variance of size-at-age in survey length-frequency distributions. - Solution: Replace the first-order upwind step with the semi-implicit Crank–Nicolson / Chang–Cooper finite-volume discretization of the Jump-Growth Fokker–Planck PDE (Datta et al. 2010, Eq. 7):

\[\frac{\partial N_i}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial (g_i N_i)}{\partial w} - \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 \left(\psi_{\text{disp}, i} \, d_i(w,t) \, N_i\right)}{\partial w^2} = -\mu_i(w,t) N_i\]

where \(d_i(w,t)\) is the mechanistic second moment of prey mass intake, optionally scaled by a species-specific dispersion multiplier \(\psi_{\text{disp}, i} \ge 1\) (analogous to Gadget’s Beta-Binomial dispersion parameter \(\beta_s\) in g3a_grow_impl_bbinom; Lentz and Elvarsson (2024)) to account for genetic growth variation and patchy foraging. - Computational Cost: Discretizing the second derivative \(\partial_w^2 (d_i N_i)\) changes the implicit linear system per species per time step from lower-bidiagonal to tridiagonal, which is still solved in exact \(O(n_w)\) operations via the Thomas algorithm (solve_tridiag) and is \(100\%\) differentiable in TMB / RTMB!

6.4.2 Pillar 2: Two-Stage Initialization (Differentiable Steady-State Spin-Up + Dynamic Projection)

In single-species assessments (SS3, SAM), the initial age vector \(N_{i,a,t_0}\) at the start of the historical time series \(t_0\) is initialized at a fished equilibrium \(N_{\text{eq}}(F_{\text{init}, i})\) plus initial deviations. For dynamic mizer: 1. Differentiable Steady-State Initialization at \(t = t_0\): Given trial parameters \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) and baseline initial fishing effort \(E_{f, t_0}\), compute the steady-state spectra \(N_{i,0}(w)\) via a fixed number (\(k = 15\text{--}25\)) of damped fixed-point iterations of the analytical steady-state envelope \(N_i(w) = N_i(w_0)\frac{g_i(w_0)}{g_i(w)}\exp\!\left(-\int_{w_0}^w \frac{\mu_i(w')}{g_i(w')} dw'\right)\) (with matching resource \(N_{R,0}(w)\)). Because a fixed number of unrolled iterations uses only elementary vector operations, TMB / RTMB differentiates smoothly through the steady-state initialization! 2. Dynamic Time-Stepping (\(t \in [t_0, T]\)): Step forward in quarterly or monthly increments \(\Delta t\) (e.g., \(\Delta t = 0.1\) or \(0.25\text{ yr}\)) driven by time-varying fishing mortality \(F_{f,i}(w, t)\), temperature anomalies \(T(t)\) (scaling metabolic rates \(k_{s,i}\), intake \(h_i\), and search rates \(\gamma_i\) via Arrhenius activation \(e^{-E_a / k_B T}\)), and state-space recruitment deviations \(\epsilon_{R,i,y}\).

6.4.3 Pillar 3: State-Space Process Random Effects (\(\mathbf{u}\))

Following SAM (Nielsen and Berg 2014) and EcoState (Thorson et al. 2024), introduce three compact sets of latent Gaussian random effects integrated out by TMB’s Laplace approximation: 1. Annual Recruitment Deviations: \[R_i(t) = R_{\text{dd}, i}(t) \exp\!\left(\epsilon_{R, i, y(t)} - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_{R, i}^2\right), \qquad \epsilon_{R, i, y} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0, \sigma_{R, i}^2\right)\] 2. Resource Spectrum Productivity / Carrying Capacity Anomalies (capturing bottom-up climate/plankton regimes): \[\kappa_R(w, t) = \kappa_0 \exp\!\left(\epsilon_{\kappa, y(t)}\right) w^{-\lambda}, \qquad \epsilon_{\kappa, y} \sim \text{AR1}\!\left(\rho_\kappa, \sigma_\kappa^2\right)\] 3. Random-Walk Fishing Effort / Mortality (when commercial effort is noisy or unrecorded): \[\ln E_{f, y+1} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\ln E_{f, y},\; \sigma_{E, f}^2\right)\]

6.4.4 Pillar 4: Observation Operators and Joint Statistical Likelihood Function

To avoid the parameter confounding identified by Punt et al. (2016), dynamic mizer should be fitted simultaneously to four orthogonal empirical data streams:

  1. Survey Biomass / Abundance Indices (\(I_{f,i,y}\), Lognormal Likelihood): Given survey selectivity \(S^{\text{surv}}_{f,i}(w)\) and catchability \(Q^{\text{surv}}_{f,i}\): \[\hat{I}_{f,i,y} = Q^{\text{surv}}_{f,i} \int_{w_0}^{w_{\max,i}} S^{\text{surv}}_{f,i}(w) \, w \, N_i(w, t_y) \, dw, \qquad \ln I^{\text{obs}}_{f,i,y} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\ln \hat{I}_{f,i,y},\; \sigma^2_{I,f,i,y}\right)\]

  2. Commercial Fishery Yields (\(Y_{f,i,y}\), Lognormal Likelihood): \[\hat{Y}_{f,i,y} = \int_{y}^{y+1} dt \int_{w_0}^{w_{\max,i}} F_{f,i}(w, t) \, w \, N_i(w, t) \, dw, \qquad \ln Y^{\text{obs}}_{f,i,y} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\ln \hat{Y}_{f,i,y},\; \sigma^2_{Y,f,i}\right)\]

  3. Survey & Commercial Length-Frequency Compositions (\(\mathbf{P}^{\text{obs}}_{f,i,y}\), Dirichlet-Multinomial or Fournier Robustified Normal Likelihood): For observed length bin \(l\) with boundaries \([L_l, L_{l+1})\) corresponding to body weights \([w_{i,l}, w_{i,l+1}) = [a_i L_l^{b_i}, a_i L_{l+1}^{b_i})\), the expected catch number in bin \(l\) is: \[\hat{C}_{f,i,l,y} = \int_{w_{i,l}}^{w_{i,l+1}} S_{f,i}(w) \, N_i(w, t_y) \, dw, \qquad \hat{p}_{f,i,l,y} = \frac{\hat{C}_{f,i,l,y}}{\sum_{m} \hat{C}_{f,i,m,y}}\] Evaluated via the self-weighting Dirichlet-Multinomial likelihood (Thorson et al. 2017) with haul sample size \(N_{\text{haul}, f, y}\) and estimated overdispersion \(\theta_{\text{comp}, f, i}\) (or Fournier’s robustified normal likelihood from MULTIFAN-CL (Fournier et al. 1998) to prevent tail outliers from distorting growth parameters).

  4. Stomach Content Diet Shares & Log-PPMR Distributions (Dirichlet-Multinomial + Gamma Likelihood): For predator \((i, w')\) in year-quarter \(t\), the model-predicted mass share of prey species \(j\) in prey weight bin \([w_{p,m}, w_{p,m+1})\) in the diet is: \[\hat{W}_{i,w', j, m, t} = \frac{\gamma_i(w') \, \theta_{ij} \int_{w_{p,m}}^{w_{p,m+1}} \phi_i(w' / w_p) \, N_j(w_p, t) \, w_p \, dw_p}{E_i(w', t)}\]

    • Taxonomic Diet Shares: Fit evacuated, CPUE-weighted (or sdmTMB-standardized; Jacobsen et al. (2023)) stomach weight proportions against \(\hat{W}_{i,w',j,m,t}\) using the SMS Dirichlet (Lewy and Vinther 2004) or Dirichlet-Multinomial (Thorson et al. 2017) likelihood, which directly identifies the interaction matrix \(\theta_{ij}\)!
    • Predator–Prey Size Kernel Parameters (\(\beta_i, \sigma_i\) or Gamma \(\alpha_i, \beta_i\)): Fit the observed log-PPMR distribution \(x = \ln(w'/w_p)\) using the Exponential Family / Gamma likelihood of Morgan (2022).

6.4.5 Pillar 5: Stabilizing Dynamic Multi-Decadal Fits via Prey Refuges & Overconsumption Caps

A major lesson from Gadget (Lentz and Elvarsson 2024), MSCAA (Curti et al. 2013), and Ecosim/EcoState (Walters et al. 1997; Thorson et al. 2024) is that during gradient optimization over a 40-year time series, trial parameter values can easily trigger runaway trophic cascades unless two stabilizing mechanisms are included: 1. Holling Type III Exponent or Foraging Arena Vulnerability: Replacing linear encounter \(\theta_{ij} N_j(w_p)\) with a weakly sigmoid Type III encounter \(\theta_{ij} N_j(w_p)^{1 + \zeta}\) (\(\zeta \approx 0.1\text{--}0.3\)) or a Foraging Arena predator-interference denominator \(\frac{\theta_{ij} v_{ij}}{2 v_{ij} + \gamma_i N_i}\) provides a low-density prey refuge and prevents unrealistic species extinctions during parameter search. 2. Smooth Differentiable Overconsumption Cap (dif_pmin): Borrowing gadget3’s smooth logistic minimum cap on total mortality per time step ensures that \(N_i(w, t)\) remains strictly positive and gradients \(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \mathcal{L}\) remain finite across the entire parameter space.
