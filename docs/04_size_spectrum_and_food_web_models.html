4  Continuous Size-Spectrum (mizer) and State-Space Food-Web Models (EwE, Rpath, EcoState)

4.1 Continuous Multispecies Size-Spectrum Models (mizer)

4.1.1 Theoretical Lineage and Core Philosophy

Size-spectrum theory originated from the empirical discovery by Sheldon et al. (1972) and Sheldon et al. (1977) that pelagic marine biomass is approximately constant across logarithmic body-mass bins (\(B(w) dw \propto w^0 d(\ln w) \implies N(w) \propto w^{-2}\)), and from the continuous mass-balance and pelagic ecosystem derivations of Platt and Denman (1977), Silvert and Platt (1978), Benoît and Rochet (2004), Andersen and Beyer (2006), and Cuesta et al. (2018).

In a landmark synthesis, Hartvig et al. (2011) and Scott et al. (2014) resolved species identity within the continuous size spectrum to create the mizer framework (Multi-species size-spectrum ecological modelling in R; applied to North Sea reference points by Blanchard et al. (2014) and reviewed comprehensively by Andersen et al. (2016), Andersen (2019), Andersen (2020), and Pennino et al. (2025)).

Unlike age-structured models where somatic growth and natural mortality are external inputs, in mizer both individual somatic growth \(g_i(w,t)\) and predation mortality \(\mu_{p,i}(w,t)\) emerge dynamically from individual-level foraging and strict conservation of mass/energy.

4.1.2 Complete Mathematical Formulation of Standard mizer

Let \(N_i(w, t)\) (\(\text{numbers} \cdot \text{g}^{-1} \cdot \text{m}^{-3}\) or total numbers per weight interval) denote the number density of species \(i \in \{1, \dots, S\}\) at body mass \(w \in [w_0, w_{\max, i}]\) and time \(t\), and let \(N_R(w, t)\) denote the background plankton/benthic resource spectrum for \(w \in [w_{\text{cut}}, w_{\text{max}, R}]\).

1. Governing McKendrick–von Foerster Conservation PDE

The time evolution of \(N_i(w, t)\) is governed by the first-order hyperbolic continuity (advection-reaction) equation (Hartvig et al. 2011, Eq. 1; Scott et al. 2014, Eq. 3.1):

\[\frac{\partial N_i(w, t)}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial \left(g_i(w, t) N_i(w, t)\right)}{\partial w} = -\mu_i(w, t) N_i(w, t)\]

coupled to the boundary condition (recruitment flux) at egg weight \(w_0\):

\[g_i(w_0, t) N_i(w_0, t) = R_{\text{dd}, i}(t)\]

2. Volumetric Search Rate, Predation Kernel, and Encounter Rate (\(E_i(w,t)\))

A predator of species \(i\) and mass \(w\) searches a water volume at allometric rate \(\gamma_i(w) = \gamma_i w^q\) (\(q \approx 0.8\)). Its size preference for prey of mass \(w_p\) is described by a predation kernel \(\phi_i(w / w_p)\), most commonly the Andersen–Ursin lognormal kernel (Scott et al. 2014, Eq. 3.4):

\[\phi_i\!\left(\frac{w}{w_p}\right) = \exp\!\left( -\frac{\left(\ln\!\left(\frac{w}{\beta_i w_p}\right)\right)^2}{2\sigma_i^2} \right)\]

(or a power-law / box / double-logistic kernel). Given the species interaction (spatial overlap / vulnerability) matrix \(\theta_{ij} \in [0, 1]\), the total biomass of suitable prey encountered per unit time \(E_i(w, t)\) by predator \((i, w)\) is:

\[E_i(w, t) = \gamma_i(w) \int \phi_i\!\left(\frac{w}{w_p}\right) \left[ \theta_{iR} N_R(w_p, t) + \sum_{j=1}^{S} \theta_{ij} N_j(w_p, t) \right] w_p \, dw_p\]

3. Feeding Level (\(f_i(w,t)\)) and Holling Type II Satiation

Given physiological maximum intake rate \(I_{\max, i}(w) = h_i w^n\) (\(n \approx 0.67\text{--}0.75\)), the dimensionless feeding level \(f_i(w, t) \in [0, 1)\) follows a Holling Type II functional response:

\[f_i(w, t) = \frac{E_i(w, t)}{E_i(w, t) + h_i w^n}\]

Thus actual biomass consumption per predator per unit time is \(C_i(w, t) = f_i(w, t) h_i w^n\).

4. Bioenergetic Allocation, Somatic Growth (\(g_i(w,t)\)), and Reproduction (\(R_{p,i}(t)\))

Assimilated energy is a fraction \(\alpha_i \in (0, 1)\) of ingested food (\(\alpha_i \approx 0.6\)), from which standard metabolic maintenance \(k_{s,i} w^p\) (\(p \approx 0.7\text{--}1.0\)) is subtracted to give net available energy \(E_{\text{avail}, i}(w, t)\):

\[E_{\text{avail}, i}(w, t) = \alpha_i \, f_i(w, t) \, h_i w^n - k_{s,i} w^p\]

A size-dependent maturation ogive \(\psi_i(w)\) (transitioning from \(0\) to \(1\) around maturation weight \(w_{\text{mat}, i}\) and scaling with \(\left(w / w_{\max, i}\right)^{1-n}\) or \(1 - (w/w_{\max,i})^{p-n}\) so growth ceases at \(w_{\max, i}\)) partitions net positive energy between somatic growth \(g_i(w, t)\) and gonadal reproduction \(E_{r,i}(w, t)\):

\[g_i(w, t) = \max\!\Big(0,\; E_{\text{avail}, i}(w, t)\Big) \Big(1 - \psi_i(w)\Big)\]

\[\psi_i(w) = \left[1 + \left(\frac{w}{w_{\text{mat}, i}}\right)^{-U}\right]^{-1} \left(\frac{w}{w_{\max, i}}\right)^{1 - n}\]

The density-independent physiological egg production rate \(R_{\text{di}, i}(t)\) (\(\text{eggs} \cdot \text{yr}^{-1}\)) is obtained by integrating reproductive energy over all sizes, multiplying by reproductive efficiency \(\epsilon_{\text{rep}, i}\) and female fraction \(1/2\), and dividing by egg weight \(w_0\):

\[R_{\text{di}, i}(t) = \frac{\epsilon_{\text{rep}, i}}{2 w_0} \int_{w_0}^{w_{\max, i}} N_i(w, t) \max\!\Big(0,\; E_{\text{avail}, i}(w, t)\Big) \psi_i(w) \, dw\]

In standard mizer, external density dependence at the larval stage (e.g., limited larval habitat or unmodeled larval mortality) is represented by passing \(R_{\text{di}, i}(t)\) through a Beverton–Holt stock-recruitment function with maximum recruitment cap \(R_{\max, i}\):

\[R_{\text{dd}, i}(t) = \frac{R_{\max, i} \, R_{\text{di}, i}(t)}{R_{\text{di}, i}(t) + R_{\max, i}}\]

5. Total Mortality (\(\mu_i(w,t)\)): Background, Predation, Starvation, and Fishing

Total instantaneous mortality \(\mu_i(w, t)\) is the sum of four mechanistic components:

\[\mu_i(w, t) = \mu_{0, i}(w) + \mu_{p, i}(w, t) + \mu_{s, i}(w, t) + F_i(w, t)\]

  1. Background / Senescence Mortality: \(\mu_{0, i}(w) = \mu_{0, i} w_{\max, i}^{n - 1}\) (allometrically scaled with \(w_{\max,i}\)).

  2. Endogenous Predation Mortality (\(\mu_{p, i}(w_p, t) \equiv M2\)): By strict mass balance, every gram of prey \((j, w_p)\) eaten by predators \((i, w)\) must appear as mortality on \((j, w_p)\) (Scott et al. 2014, Eq. 3.11):

    \[\mu_{p, j}(w_p, t) = \sum_{i=1}^{S} \theta_{ij} \int_{w_0}^{w_{\max, i}} \phi_i\!\left(\frac{w}{w_p}\right) \Big(1 - f_i(w, t)\Big) \gamma_i(w) \, N_i(w, t) \, dw\]

    (Notice the identity \(\frac{f_i(w) h_i w^n}{E_i(w)} = 1 - f_i(w)\): when a predator is satiated [\(f_i(w) \to 1\)], its per-unit-prey clearance rate \((1 - f_i)\gamma_i(w)\) drops toward zero!)

  3. Starvation Mortality (\(\mu_{s, i}(w, t)\)): Whenever available assimilated energy falls below metabolic maintenance (\(E_{\text{avail}, i}(w, t) < 0\)), individuals suffer starvation mortality proportional to the energy deficit divided by lipid energy stores \(\xi_{\text{store}} w\) (Scott et al. 2014, Eq. 3.12):

    \[\mu_{s, i}(w, t) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{-E_{\text{avail}, i}(w, t)}{\xi_{\text{store}} w} & \text{if } E_{\text{avail}, i}(w, t) < 0 \\[8pt] 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}\]

  4. Fishing Mortality (\(F_i(w, t)\)): Summed over fishing gears/fleets \(f\) with size selectivity \(S_{f,i}(w)\), catchability \(Q_{f,i}\), and fishing effort \(E_{f}(t)\):

    \[F_i(w, t) = \sum_{f} S_{f, i}(w) \, Q_{f, i} \, E_{f}(t)\]

    generating total fishery yield in weight \(Y_{f,i}(t) = \int_{w_0}^{w_{\max,i}} F_{f,i}(w, t) \, w \, N_i(w, t) \, dw\).

6. Resource Spectrum Dynamics (\(N_R(w,t)\))

The background plankton/benthos spectrum \(N_R(w, t)\) evolves via semi-chemostat (or logistic) renewal at rate \(r_R(w) = r_0 w^{n-1}\) toward carrying capacity \(\kappa_R(w) = \kappa w^{-\lambda}\) (\(\lambda \approx 2 + q - n \approx 2.05\)):

\[\frac{\partial N_R(w, t)}{\partial t} = r_R(w) \Big(\kappa_R(w) - N_R(w, t)\Big) - \mu_{p, R}(w, t) N_R(w, t)\]

7. Semi-Implicit Upwind Finite-Difference Discretization in mizer

On a logarithmically spaced weight grid \(w_j = w_0 \cdot 10^{(j-1)\Delta x}\) with bin widths \(\Delta w_j = w_{j+1} - w_j\), mizer solves the McKendrick–von Foerster PDE over time step \(\Delta t\) by evaluating encounter \(E_i\), feeding level \(f_i\), growth \(g_{i,j}^t\), and mortality \(\mu_{i,j}^t\) explicitly at time \(t\) (using Fast Fourier Transform [FFT] convolution on the log-weight grid for the integrals \(E_i\) and \(\mu_{p,i}\) when kernels are shift-invariant in \(\ln(w/w_p)\)), and then advancing \(N_{i,j}^{t+\Delta t}\) via a first-order upwind, backward-Euler (semi-implicit) scheme (Scott et al. 2014, Appendix A):

\[\frac{N_{i,j}^{t+\Delta t} - N_{i,j}^t}{\Delta t} + \frac{g_{i,j}^t N_{i,j}^{t+\Delta t} - g_{i,j-1}^t N_{i,j-1}^{t+\Delta t}}{\Delta w_j} = -\mu_{i,j}^t N_{i,j}^{t+\Delta t}\]

Solving for \(N_{i,j}^{t+\Delta t}\) gives a lower-bidiagonal linear system solved in \(O(n_w)\) forward substitution:

\[N_{i,j}^{t+\Delta t} = \frac{N_{i,j}^t + \frac{\Delta t}{\Delta w_j} g_{i,j-1}^t N_{i,j-1}^{t+\Delta t}}{1 + \Delta t \left(\frac{g_{i,j}^t}{\Delta w_j} + \mu_{i,j}^t\right)}\]

4.2 The Jump-Growth Fokker–Planck PDE (Datta, Delius & Law 2010, 2011)

4.2.1 Why the First-Order McKendrick–von Foerster PDE Fails for Dynamic Cohort Fitting

In the standard McKendrick–von Foerster PDE \(\partial_t N + \partial_w (g N) = -\mu N\), somatic growth is treated as a purely deterministic continuous drift \(dw/dt = g(w, t)\). Consequently: 1. No Intrinsic Cohort Broadening: All individuals born at the same instant \(t_0\) at egg weight \(w_0\) travel along the exact same characteristic trajectory \(w(t)\) with zero variance in size-at-age! If recruitment is pulsed seasonally (or fluctuates interannually as in real stock assessments), a narrow delta-function pulse at \(w_0\) remains an unrealistically sharp spike (broadened only by numerical discretization error \(\propto \Delta w_j\) of the upwind scheme!). 2. Grid-Dependent Numerical Diffusion: In mizer’s first-order upwind scheme, changing the number of weight bins \(n_w\) or time step \(\Delta t\) alters the numerical diffusivity \(D_{\text{num}} \approx \frac{1}{2} g \Delta w\), which artificially changes how recruitment pulses smear across length compositions! 3. Deterministic Instability of the Community Size Spectrum: Without growth dispersion or an external stock-recruitment clamp, the continuous McKendrick–von Foerster PDE exhibits traveling-wave instabilities (cohort cycles) for realistic predator-prey mass ratios (\(\beta \sim 100\); Datta et al. (2011)).

4.2.2 Derivation of the Jump-Growth Equation (Datta, Delius & Law 2010)

Datta et al. (2010) resolved this fundamental limitation from first principles by observing that fish do not grow by absorbing a continuous fluid: they grow by discrete stochastic jumps \(\Delta w = \alpha w_p\) each time they capture and assimilate a discrete prey organism of body mass \(w_p\).

Starting from the exact Markov Master Equation for discrete prey-capture events in continuous time (Datta et al. 2010, Eq. 5): \[\frac{\partial N_i(w,t)}{\partial t} = \int dw_p \Big[ \text{Transition in from } (w - \alpha_i w_p) - \text{Transition out from } w \Big] - \mu_i(w,t) N_i(w,t)\] and performing a second-order Kramers–Moyal Taylor expansion in the prey mass increment \(\alpha_i w_p \ll w\), Datta et al. (2010) derived the Jump-Growth Fokker–Planck (Advection–Diffusion–Reaction) PDE:

\[\frac{\partial N_i(w, t)}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial \left(g_i(w, t) N_i(w, t)\right)}{\partial w} = \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 \left(d_i(w, t) N_i(w, t)\right)}{\partial w^2} - \mu_i(w, t) N_i(w, t)\]

where: - The first-order drift (mean somatic growth rate) \(g_i(w, t)\) is the first moment of assimilated prey mass: \[g_i(w, t) = \alpha_i \Big(1 - \psi_i(w)\Big) \Big(1 - f_i(w, t)\Big) \gamma_i(w) \int \phi_i\!\left(\frac{w}{w_p}\right) N_c(w_p, t) \, \mathbf{w_p} \, dw_p - k_{s,i} w^p \Big(1 - \psi_i(w)\Big)\] - The second-order macro-diffusion (growth variance) coefficient \(d_i(w, t)\) is the second moment of assimilated prey mass (Datta et al. 2010, Eq. 9): \[d_i(w, t) = \alpha_i^2 \Big(1 - \psi_i(w)\Big)^2 \Big(1 - f_i(w, t)\Big) \gamma_i(w) \int \phi_i\!\left(\frac{w}{w_p}\right) N_c(w_p, t) \, \mathbf{w_p^2} \, dw_p\]

[!IMPORTANT] Mathematical & Practical Significance of the Jump-Growth Diffusion Term \(d_i(w,t)\): 1. Zero Free Parameters: In the pure mechanistic derivation, \(d_i(w, t)\) requires no additional free parameters (or a single scaling multiplier if accounting for behavioral bout-feeding / schooling variation, analogous to Gadget’s Beta-Binomial \(\beta_s\)). 2. Physical Cohort Dispersion: A recruiting year-class pulse at \(w_0\) naturally broadens into a realistic bell-shaped size-at-age distribution as it advects to larger sizes, enabling direct likelihood fitting to time-series length-frequency distributions! 3. Grid-Independent Convergence: When solved on a sufficiently fine grid (or via Crank–Nicolson / implicit central differences for the \(\partial_w^2(d_i N_i)\) term), the physical diffusivity \(d_i(w, t)\) dominates numerical grid diffusivity, making model predictions invariant to grid resolution.

4.3 Population Regulation Without Ad-Hoc Stock–Recruitment Relationships (Canales, Delius & Law 2020)

In standard fisheries models (including standard mizer, SMS, Gadget, and LeMaRns), population abundance is stabilized externally by imposing an ad-hoc Beverton–Holt or Ricker stock-recruitment relationship (SRR) at the boundary \(w_0\), with one or two free species-specific parameters (\(R_{\max, i}\) or steepness \(h_i\)) that have little direct physiological basis and strongly dictate MSY predictions.

Canales et al. (2020) demonstrated that in a size-spectrum model with linear density-independent reproduction (\(R_{\text{dd}, i} = R_{\text{di}, i}\), i.e., \(R_{\max, i} \to \infty\)), three endogenous biological mechanisms naturally regulate population abundance and coexistence without any ad-hoc SRR: 1. Density-Dependent Somatic Growth via Prey Competition: When a species’ abundance increases, it depletes its prey size range, lowering its feeding level \(f_i(w, t)\) and slowing juvenile growth \(g_i(w, t)\). Because juveniles spend longer in small vulnerable size classes (\(w < w_{\text{mat}, i}\)) while experiencing predation mortality \(\mu_{p,i}(w, t)\), cumulative survival to maturity \(\exp\!\left(-\int_{w_0}^{w_{\text{mat}}} \frac{\mu_i(w)}{g_i(w)} dw\right)\) drops sharply! 2. Early-Life Predation and Cannibalism: Extending the predation kernel to planktonic/larval stages and accounting for intra- and inter-specific predation on larvae creates a self-regulating mortality bottleneck. 3. Reproductive Allometry (\(m_i > n_i\)): When reproductive investment scales steeper with body mass than metabolic intake (matching empirical BOFFFF—Big Old Fat Fecund Female—observations), truncating the adult size structure via fishing directly reduces per-biomass egg output without needing an SRR curve.

4.4 Mass-Balance, Foraging Arena, and State-Space Food-Web Models (Ecopath, Ecosim, Rpath, EcoState)

While size-spectrum models resolve continuous body mass, the most widely applied whole-ecosystem modeling framework in marine science is Ecopath with Ecosim (EwE)—originating with Polovina (1984) and Christensen and Pauly (1992), extended dynamically and spatially via Foraging Arena Theory in Ecosim and Ecospace by Walters et al. (1997), Walters et al. (1999), Christensen and Walters (2004), Christensen et al. (2005), and Christensen et al. (2024), critically evaluated by Plagányi and Butterworth (2004), and automated via stepwise fitting by Scott et al. (2016)—along with its reproducible R implementation Rpath (Lucey et al. 2020) and its recent TMB state-space stock assessment reformulation EcoState (Thorson et al. 2024).

4.4.1 Ecopath: Static Mass Balance at Reference Equilibrium

Ecopath partitions the ecosystem into \(i \in \{1, \dots, n\}\) functional groups (which may be single species, multi-species guilds, or juvenile/adult life stanzas). At a reference equilibrium period, production of group \(i\) equals the sum of all mortality and export fluxes (Christensen and Walters 2004, Eq. 1; Lucey et al. 2020, Eq. 1):

\[B_i \left(\frac{P}{B}\right)_i \cdot EE_i = Y_i + E_i + BA_i + \sum_{j=1}^{n} B_j \left(\frac{Q}{B}\right)_j DC_{ij}\]

where: - \(B_i\) is biomass, \((P/B)_i = Z_i\) is the production-to-biomass ratio (total mortality rate under equilibrium), and \((Q/B)_j\) is the consumption-to-biomass ratio of predator \(j\), - \(EE_i \in [0, 1]\) is Ecotrophic Efficiency (the fraction of production explained within the modeled system, so \(M0_i = (P/B)_i (1 - EE_i)\) is residual unexplained mortality), - \(Y_i\) is fishery catch, \(E_i\) is net migration, \(BA_i\) is biomass accumulation, and - \(DC_{ij}\) is the diet composition matrix (fraction by weight of predator \(j\)’s diet consisting of prey \(i\), with \(\sum_i DC_{ij} = 1\)). For each group \(i\), three of the four core parameters \(\{B_i, (P/B)_i, (Q/B)_i, EE_i\}\) are supplied along with \(DC_{ij}\) and \(Y_i\), and the linear system is solved for the missing parameter (typically \(EE_i\) or \(B_i\)).

4.4.2 Ecosim and Walters’ Foraging Arena Theory

When Ecopath is projected dynamically through time as a system of coupled ODEs (Ecosim; Walters et al. (1997)):

\[\frac{dB_i}{dt} = g_i \sum_{j} Q_{ji}(t) - \sum_{k} Q_{ik}(t) + I_i - \left(M0_i + F_i(t) + e_i\right) B_i(t)\]

where \(g_i = (P/Q)_i\) is net growth efficiency, a critical question arises: how should the consumption flux \(Q_{ij}(t)\) of prey \(i\) by predator \(j\) depend on \(B_i(t)\) and \(B_j(t)\)?

Walters et al. (1997) showed that if one uses standard mass-action Lotka–Volterra (\(Q_{ij} = a_{ij} B_i B_j\)) or standard Holling Type II in a complex 30-species food web, the ecosystem rapidly collapses via Gause competitive exclusion or violent predator-prey limit cycles.

To resolve this, Walters et al. (1997) formulated Foraging Arena Theory: at any given moment, total prey biomass \(B_i\) is partitioned with respect to predator \(j\) into an invulnerable (refuge/sheltered/un-overlapping) state \(B_i - V_{ij}\) and a small vulnerable foraging arena state \(V_{ij}\), with behavioural/spatial exchange rate \(v_{ij}\) between them:

\[\frac{dV_{ij}}{dt} = v_{ij}\left(B_i - V_{ij}\right) - v'_{ij} V_{ij} - a_{ij} V_{ij} B_j\]

Assuming the behavioral exchange \(V_{ij}\) equilibrates rapidly relative to annual population changes (\(dV_{ij}/dt \approx 0\), with \(v'_{ij} = v_{ij}\)), solving for \(V_{ij}\) yields the Foraging Arena Functional Response (Walters et al. 1997, Eq. 4; Christensen and Walters 2004, Eq. 5; Thorson et al. 2024, Eq. 4):

\[Q_{ij}(t) = a_{ij} V_{ij}(t) B_j(t) = \frac{a_{ij} \, v_{ij} \, B_i(t) \, B_j(t)}{2 v_{ij} + a_{ij} B_j(t)}\]

Calibrating to the Ecopath baseline \(Q_{ij,0} = B_{j,0} (Q/B)_{j,0} DC_{ij,0}\) at reference biomasses \((B_{i,0}, B_{j,0})\) eliminates \(a_{ij}\) in favor of a single dimensionless vulnerability multiplier \(X_{ij} = \frac{v_{ij} B_{i,0}}{Q_{ij,0}} \in (1, \infty)\) (default \(X_{ij} = 2\)):

\[Q_{ij}(t) = Q_{ij,0} \, \frac{X_{ij}}{X_{ij} - 1 + \frac{B_j(t)}{B_{j,0}}} \, \frac{B_i(t)}{B_{i,0}}\]

  • Low Vulnerability (\(X_{ij} \to 1\), “Bottom-Up / Donor Control”): As \(X_{ij} \to 1\), \(Q_{ij}(t) \to Q_{ij,0} \frac{B_i(t)}{B_{i,0}}\) is independent of predator biomass \(B_j(t)\)! Doubling predator abundance \(B_j\) simply means twice as many predators compete for the same trickle of prey entering the foraging arena, halving per-capita predator intake and preventing top-down prey extermination.
  • High Vulnerability (\(X_{ij} \to \infty\), “Top-Down / Lotka–Volterra Control”): As \(X_{ij} \to \infty\), \(Q_{ij}(t) \to Q_{ij,0} \frac{B_i(t)}{B_{i,0}} \frac{B_j(t)}{B_{j,0}}\) recovers mass-action top-down predation.

(For critique of EwE’s delay-difference multi-stanza assumptions and vulnerability overfitting when many \(X_{ij}\) are tuned to short time series, see Plagányi and Butterworth (2004) and the stepwise AIC fitting protocol of Scott et al. (2016)).

4.4.3 EcoState (Thorson et al. 2024): State-Space Mass-Balance Assessment in TMB

Historically, EwE was fitted by shooting (deterministic trajectory matching) without process errors or formal state-space marginal likelihoods. In 2024, James Thorson, Kirstin Holsman, Gaichas, Aydin, and Punt introduced EcoState (Thorson et al. 2024), which embeds the Ecopath + Ecosim ODEs directly inside a Template Model Builder (TMB) state-space stock assessment model:

  1. Continuous-Time ODE with Annual Process Errors \(\epsilon_{i,t}\): Within each year \(t \in \{t_0, \dots, T\}\), biomass \(B_i(s)\) evolves over \(s \in [t, t+1]\) via the Ecosim Foraging Arena ODE augmented with an estimated annual unexplained process-error rate \(\epsilon_{i,t}\) (Thorson et al. 2024, Eq. 5): \[\frac{dB_i(s)}{ds} = g_i \sum_j Q_{ji}(s) - \sum_k Q_{ik}(s) - \Big(M0_i + F_{i,t} - \epsilon_{i,t}\Big) B_i(s)\] integrated numerically in TMB using a differentiable Runge–Kutta / Adams–Bashforth ODE solver (odeint), where \(\epsilon_{i,t} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2_{\text{proc}, i})\) are random effects marginalized via the Laplace approximation.
  2. Simultaneous Joint Likelihood Across All Data Streams: EcoState estimates Ecopath baseline parameters (\(\ln B_{i,0}, EE_i, DC_{ij,0}\)), Ecosim vulnerabilities \(\ln(X_{ij}-1)\), fishing mortalities \(\ln F_{i,t}\), and process error variances \(\sigma_{\text{proc},i}\) simultaneously by fitting to:
    • Survey biomass indices: \(\ln I_{i,t} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\ln(q_i \hat{B}_{i,t}),\, \sigma^2_{I,i,t}\right)\),
