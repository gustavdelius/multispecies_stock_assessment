1  Taxonomy and Theoretical Foundations of Multispecies Stock Assessment Models

1.1 The Complexity Spectrum: Strategic Models, Tactical Assessments, and MICE

Fisheries ecosystem models span a wide continuum of structural complexity, taxonomic resolution, and statistical rigor (Plagányi et al. 2014; Collie et al. 2016; Karp et al. 2023):

1.1.1 Models of Intermediate Complexity for Ecosystem Assessments (MICE)

(Plagányi et al. 2014) and (Collie et al. 2016) formalized the concept of MICE (Models of Intermediate Complexity for Ecosystem assessments) as the “sweet spot” for tactical fisheries management. As model complexity increases, structural bias (from omitting trophic interactions) decreases, whereas parameter estimation uncertainty (from fitting hundreds of poorly identified interaction rates to noisy data) increases rapidly (Collie et al. 2016, fig. 1).

A model qualifies as a tactical multispecies assessment or MICE when it satisfies four operational criteria (Plagányi et al. 2014; Karp et al. 2023):

  1. Context-Driven Taxonomic Scope: It models only the core interacting species (typically \(3\text{--}12\) commercial predator and prey stocks plus \(1\text{--}3\) aggregated “other food” or resource pools) responsible for the majority of predation mortality and fishery yield.
  2. Formal Statistical Estimation: Parameters are estimated by maximizing a formal likelihood (or posterior density) against empirical time series of commercial catches, scientific survey indices, age/size compositions, and stomach contents, rather than relying purely on literature plug-in values or manual tuning.
  3. Diagnostic and Uncertainty Quantification: It provides standard stock assessment diagnostics (residual patterns, retrospective Mohn’s \(\rho\), profile likelihoods, Hessian/Laplace standard errors, or MCMC/SMC credible intervals).
  4. Operational Cadence: It can be updated on the 1–5 year assessment cycle used by management bodies (e.g., ICES Working Group on Multispecies Assessment Methods [WGSAM] key runs supplying time-varying \(M = M1 + M2\) matrices to single-species SAM assessments, or NOAA NPFMC Bering Sea CEATTLE runs).

1.2 Fundamental Axes of Structural Classification

Every multispecies stock assessment model can be positioned along three fundamental mathematical axes (Stefánsson and Pálsson 1998; Andersen et al. 2016; Pennino et al. 2025) (see Table 1.1):

1.2.1 Axis 1: Individual State Space (Age vs. Length vs. Continuous Body Mass)

  1. Biomass Pool / Multi-Stanza (\(B_i(t)\)):

    • Governing Dynamical Equation: Coupled system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs):

      \[\frac{dB_i}{dt} = g_i \sum_j Q_{ij} - \sum_k Q_{ki} - (M0_i + F_i) B_i \tag{1.1}\]

    • Strengths & Limitations: Fast; low state dimension; captures whole-food-web energy flow; cannot directly fit length or age compositions without multi-stanza delay-difference approximations.

  2. Discrete Age-Structured (\(N_{i,a,t}\)):
    • Governing Dynamical Equation: Difference equation along cohort diagonals with empirical weight-at-age \(w_{i,a,t}\): \[N_{i,a+1,t+1} = N_{i,a,t} \exp(-Z_{i,a,t}) \tag{1.2}\]
    • Strengths & Limitations: Directly compatible with single-species age-based assessments; simple cohort tracking; requires aging data (otoliths); size-based predation must be mapped through fixed \(w_{i,a,t}\) or age-length keys.
  3. Joint Age-Length Structured (\(N_{i,a,l,t}\) and \(W_{i,a,l,t}\)):
    • Governing Dynamical Equation: Advection-dispersion across length bins within each discrete age class: \[\mathbf{N}_{i,a,t+\Delta t} = \mathbf{G}_{i,a,t} \mathbf{S}_{i,a,t} \mathbf{N}_{i,a,t} \tag{1.3}\]
    • Strengths & Limitations: Separates chronological age from physical size; resolves size-selective mortality effects on mean length-at-age; computationally intensive due to the \((s \times a \times l \times r)\) state tensor.
  4. Discrete Length-Structured / Age-Free (\(N_{i,l,t}\)):
    • Governing Dynamical Equation: Matrix projection across length bins: \[\mathbf{N}_{i,t+1} = \mathbf{X}_i \mathbf{S}_{i,t} \mathbf{N}_{i,t} + \mathbf{R}_{i,t} \tag{1.4}\]
    • Strengths & Limitations: Requires no otolith aging; matches survey length-frequency data directly; deterministic fractional bin transition (\(\phi_{i,l}\)) introduces numerical cohort diffusion unless a stochastic transition matrix \(\mathbf{X}_{l,l'}\) is used.
  5. Continuous Body-Mass Spectrum (\(N_i(w,t)\), \(\text{numbers}\cdot\text{g}^{-1}\)):
    • Governing Dynamical Equation: McKendrick–von Foerster PDE (or Fokker–Planck Jump-Growth PDE): \[\frac{\partial N_i}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial (g_i N_i)}{\partial w} = \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2 (d_i N_i)}{\partial w^2} - \mu_i N_i \tag{1.5}\]
    • Strengths & Limitations: Mechanistic mass and energy conservation from plankton to top predators; emergent growth, maturity, and mortality; requires PDE discretization and diffusion treatment to track cohorts dynamically.

1.2.2 Axis 2: Direction of Trophic Feedback (One-Way vs. Two-Way Coupling)

A critical distinction in multispecies stock assessment—emphasized by (Stefánsson and Pálsson 1998) and (Andersen et al. 2016)—is whether trophic interactions operate one-way (top-down only) or two-way (top-down predation mortality + bottom-up bioenergetic growth/reproduction):

One-Way Trophic Feedback (“Predation-Only” / Fixed-Ration Models)

Used in MSVPA, SMS, LeMans/LeMaRns, and standard modes of MSCAA/Hydra. Predator abundance determines the predation mortality rate \(M2\) experienced by prey: \[\text{Predator Abundance } N_{\text{pred}} \implies \text{Prey Mortality } M2_{\text{prey}} \tag{1.6}\]

However, predator somatic growth rate, weight-at-age \(w_{a,t}\), and per-capita total food consumption rate \(R_{a,t}\) are independent of prey abundance. Even if all modeled fish prey collapse, predators maintain their prescribed ration \(R_{a,t}\) (implicitly compensating by consuming an inexhaustible “other food” pool \(B_{\text{other}}\)) and follow fixed growth curves.

WarningManagement Consequence of One-Way Coupling
In forward projections under high forage-fish fishing mortality, one-way (predation-only) models predict that piscivores suffer zero loss of somatic growth or reproductive output when their prey are fished down (Jacobsen et al. 2016; Pope et al. 2019).

Two-Way Trophic Feedback (“Bioenergetic / Size-Spectrum” Models)

  • Used in mizer, MULTSPEC/Gadget (when configured with g3a_grow_length_multspec or g3a_grow_weight_weightjones), bioenergetic CEATTLE, and Ecosim/EcoState.
  • Every predation event simultaneously removes biomass from the prey cohort (\(M2_{\text{prey}}\)) and supplies assimilable energy to the predator cohort, driving somatic growth \(g_{\text{pred}}\), energy reserves/condition \(W_{\text{pred}}\), egg production \(R_{p,\text{pred}}\), and (when energy intake falls below metabolic maintenance) starvation mortality \(\mu_{s,\text{pred}}\): \[\text{Prey Abundance } N_{\text{prey}} \Longleftrightarrow \begin{cases} \text{Top-Down: Predation Mortality } M2_{\text{prey}} \\ \text{Bottom-Up: Predator Growth } g_{\text{pred}}, \text{ Reproduction } R_{p,\text{pred}}, \text{ Starvation } \mu_{s,\text{pred}} \end{cases} \tag{1.7}\]

1.2.3 Axis 3: Calibration Paradigm (Deterministic VPA vs. Pattern-Oriented/History Matching vs. Integrated Statistical Likelihood)

  1. Deterministic / Iterative Backward VPA (MSVPA): Assumes catch-at-age and stomach contents are known without error; solves for historical \(N_{a,t}\), \(F_{a,t}\), and \(M2_{a,t}\) via fixed-point iteration.
  2. Equilibrium / Pattern-Oriented / History Matching (mizer steady-state, LeMaRns SMC, EwE): Calibrates time-averaged catch or biomass against a steady-state reference period, or uses rejection/Sequential Monte Carlo sampling with implausibility metrics (Spence, Thorpe, et al. 2021) to identify ensembles of plausible parameter vectors.
  3. Integrated Maximum Likelihood / State-Space Assessment (SMS, Gadget/gadget3, CEATTLE, MSCAA/Hydra, EcoState): Formulates a differentiable forward process model coupled to explicit statistical observation models for each raw data stream (commercial catch-at-age/length, survey CPUE indices, stomach content proportions), optimizing the joint log-likelihood (or marginal Laplace likelihood in TMB) via automatic differentiation.

1.3 The Andersen & Ursin (1977) Theoretical Foundation and Livingston’s (1983) Evaluation

Virtually all modern size- and age-structured multispecies models—including MSVPA, SMS, Gadget, LeMans/LeMaRns, CSMS, and mizer—trace their mathematical lineage to the landmark monograph by K. P. Andersen and Erik Ursin (1977), “A Multispecies Extension to the Beverton and Holt Theory of Fishing, with Accounts of Phosphorus Circulation and Primary Production”, Ursin’s (1973) empirical analysis of prey size preferences in cod and dab, and Patricia Livingston’s (1983) mathematical evaluation and sensitivity analysis of the Andersen–Ursin equations for North Pacific fisheries.

Because (Andersen and Ursin 1977) is the shared ancestor of both statistical multispecies assessments (SMS, MSCAA, CEATTLE) and continuous size-spectrum models (mizer), examining its exact differential equations and parameter sensitivities—as documented by (Livingston 1983, 2–11, Table 1)—clarifies how these two branches diverged.

1.3.1 Core Coupled Differential Equations of Andersen & Ursin (1977)

(Andersen and Ursin 1977) extended the three basic (Beverton and Holt 1957) differential equations for individual body weight \(w_i(t)\), cohort abundance \(N_i(t)\), and fishery yield \(Y_i(t)\) of computational entity \(i\) (a species-age group) (Livingston 1983, Eqs. 1–8):

  1. Bioenergetic Pütter / Generalized von Bertalanffy Somatic Growth: The rate of food consumption \(\frac{dR_i}{dt}\) by an individual of entity \(i\) with body mass \(w_i\) is proportional to its allometric absorbing surface \(w_i^m\) and its dimensionless feeding level \(f_i \in [0, 1]\) (capped in the full model at a critical feeding level \(f_c = 0.44\)): \[\frac{dR_i}{dt} = f_i \, h_i \, w_i^m \qquad (0 \le f_i \le f_c \le 1) \tag{1.8}\] Only a fraction \(v_i = (1 - \alpha)\beta\) of consumed food is net-assimilated into tissue, where \(\beta = 0.80\) is the digested fraction of food and \(\alpha = 0.40\) is the fraction of digested energy lost in specific dynamic action / energy transfer costs (so \(v_i = 0.48\) universally; (Livingston 1983, Table 1, p. 11)). Subtracting fasting metabolic catabolism \(k_i w_i^n\) gives the individual weight ODE: \[\frac{dw_i}{dt} = v_i \frac{dR_i}{dt} - k_i w_i^n = (1 - \alpha)\beta \, f_i \, h_i \, w_i^m - k_i w_i^n \tag{1.9}\] where (Andersen and Ursin 1977) estimated universal exponents \(m = 0.56\) (or \(m = 2/3\) in the simplified skeleton model) and \(n = 0.83\) (or \(n = 1\) in the skeleton model) across 81 fish species (Ursin 1967).

  2. Ursin’s (1973) Lognormal Size-Preference Kernel and Suitability (\(G_{ij}\)): Analyzing thousands of stomach records of cod (Gadus morhua) and dab (Limanda limanda), (Ursin 1973, Eq. 2) demonstrated that predator \(i\) of weight \(w_i\) evaluates a potential prey item \(j\) of weight \(w_j\) according to a symmetric Gaussian distribution on the logarithmic predator-prey mass ratio \(\ln(w_i / w_j)\): \[g_{ij}(w_i, w_j) = \exp\!\left( -\frac{\left(\ln\frac{w_i}{w_j} - \eta_i\right)^2}{2\sigma_i^2} \right) \tag{1.10}\] where \(\eta_i\) is the preferred log predator-prey weight ratio (“a prey twice the preferred size is as acceptable as a prey half the preferred size”; (Livingston 1983, 3, Fig. 1)) and \(\sigma_i\) is the standard deviation of diet breadth. Multiplying by species-specific behavioral/habitat vulnerability \(\rho_{ij} \in (0, 1]\) gives total suitability: \[G_{ij} = \rho_{ij} \, g_{ij}(w_i, w_j) \tag{1.11}\]

  3. Available Food (\(\Psi_i\)), Search Volume (\(q_i w_i^r\)), and Feeding Level (\(f_i\)): Total suitable food biomass encountered by predator \(i\) is \(\Psi_i = \sum_j \Psi_{ij} = \sum_j G_{ij} w_j N_j\). The feeding level \(f_i\) follows a Holling Type II saturation curve (Livingston 1983, Eqs. 5–6): \[f_i = \frac{\Psi_i}{\Psi_i + Q_i}, \qquad Q_i = \frac{V \text{ (or } A\text{)}}{q_i \, w_i^r} \tag{1.12}\] where \(V\) (pelagic water volume) or \(A\) (demersal seabed area) scales the half-saturation food biomass \(Q_i\), \(r = 0.15\) is the universal search rate exponent, and \(q_i\) is the search rate coefficient (\(23\text{ m}^3\text{ g}^{-(1+r)}\) for pelagic habitat or \(0.53\text{ m}^2\text{ g}^{-(1+r)}\) for demersal habitat; (Livingston 1983, Table 1, p. 10)).

  4. Cohort Mortality Partitioning (\(F_i\), \(\hat{M}_i\), \(M2_i\)) and Discrete Spawning: Cohort numbers \(N_i(t)\) decay according to (Livingston 1983, Eqs. 2A, 8): \[\frac{dN_i}{dt} = -\left(F_i + \hat{M}_i + M2_i\right) N_i, \qquad \frac{dY_i}{dt} = F_i \, N_i \, w_i \tag{1.13}\] where predation mortality \(M2_i\) inflicted on prey \(i\) by all predator groups \(j\) is the ratio of total biomass of \(i\) consumed per unit time to the standing biomass \(N_i w_i\) of \(i\): \[M2_i = \frac{1}{N_i w_i} \sum_{j} \frac{dR_j}{dt} \, N_j \, \frac{\Psi_{ji}}{\Psi_j} \tag{1.14}\] Residual natural mortality \(\hat{M}_i = M1_i + M4_i + M5_i + M6_i\) comprises density-dependent larval mortality \(M1_i\) plus background disease (\(M4_i \approx 0.03\text{ yr}^{-1}\)), senescence (\(M5_i\)), and spawning stress (\(M6_i\)). At discrete spawning dates, mature age groups lose a weight fraction \(\pi_i\) to release \(E = \sum \pi_i(1-\pi_{\text{male}}) N_i w_i / \omega_i\) eggs of weight \(\omega_i\), which hatch into age-0 recruits via a Beverton–Holt density-dependent survival function (Livingston 1983, Eq. 9, p. 13): \[E1 = E \left(\frac{C}{C + E}\right) \tag{1.15}\]

1.3.2 Nonlinear Parameter Sensitivity of the Andersen–Ursin System (Livingston 1983)

By perturbing each parameter of the 14-entity Andersen–Ursin skeleton model (phytoplankton, 3 zooplankton age classes, 4 herring age classes, 6 cod age classes) by \(\pm 10\%\) and \(\pm 20\%\) and fitting the nonlinear power-law response \(y = \alpha x^\beta\) (Livingston 1983, 13–27, Tables 4–6), Livingston discovered a striking hierarchy of parameter sensitivities:

  • Low Sensitivity (\(|\beta| \le 0.5\), Table 4): Spawning fraction \(\pi\), residual natural mortality \(M1\), fish egg weights \(\omega_2, \omega_3\), and herring fishing mortalities \(F_6\text{--}F_8\) had weak, nearly linear effects on equilibrium numbers and body weights.
  • Moderate Sensitivity (\(0.5 < |\beta| \le 1.5\), Table 5): Predator search rate scalar \(Q\), density-dependent larval recruitment caps \(C_2, C_3\), and adult cod fishing mortality \(F_{14}\) had moderate effects; increasing \(Q\) (i.e., reducing search efficiency \(q_i\)) lowered individual body weights \(W_i\) (\(\beta = -0.34\) to \(-0.66\)) while increasing prey numbers \(N_i\) (\(\beta = +0.12\) to \(+0.53\)) via reduced predation.
  • High, Strongly Nonlinear Sensitivity (\(|\beta| > 1.5\), Table 6): The anabolic growth and assimilation parameters (\(v, H_1, H_2, H_3, m\)), catabolic exponent (\(n\)), and mean prey size preference (\(\eta\)) produced massive, strongly nonlinear responses across the food web (e.g., \(\beta = +7.17\) for total weight with respect to anabolic exponent \(m\), and \(\beta = +2.39\text{ to }+3.37\) for cod weight with respect to assimilation efficiency \(v\)).
  • Mathematical Rationale: In a two-way size-structured model, increasing anabolic growth (\(H_i, m, v\)) simultaneously (1) increases predator body mass \(w_j\), (2) increases per-capita predator ration \(h_j w_j^m\), and (3) shifts the predator’s preferred prey size window \(w_j e^{-\eta_j}\) across the prey size spectrum, creating a multiplicative trophic cascade across cohorts (Livingston 1983, 26–27).

1.3.3 The Fundamental Divergence: How SMS/MSVPA vs. mizer Simplified Andersen & Ursin (1977)

The MSVPA / SMS Simplification (Helgason and Gislason 1979; Pope 1979; Lewy and Vinther 2004)

In the late 1970s, the ICES Multispecies Working Group realized that Andersen & Ursin’s full 83-ODE system had too many unknown bioenergetic parameters to estimate directly from fisheries data, unless one assumed that feeding level \(f_j\) (and thus total consumption rate \(R_j(w_j) = f_j h_j w_j^n\) and somatic growth \(w_{a,t}\)) was known externally from gastric evacuation studies and commercial weight-at-age sampling.

Under this assumption, the fraction of predator \(j\)’s diet consisting of prey \((i, w_i)\) is simply the ratio of suitable prey biomass to total suitable food: \[\text{Diet Proportion } D_{ij} = \frac{G_{ij} B_i}{\Phi_j} \tag{1.16}\]

Consequently, the instantaneous predation mortality rate \(M2_i\) on prey \(i\) simplifies to: \[M2_i = \sum_{j} \frac{\bar{N}_j \, R_j \, G_{ij}}{\Phi_j} \tag{1.17}\]

NoteKey Historical Insight: The MSVPA Simplification
This simplification made \(G_{ij}/\Phi_j\) directly observable in stomach content data (since stomach content weight proportions after evacuation correction estimate \(D_{ij}\)), launching the entire MSVPA and SMS assessment tradition. However, the price paid was the loss of dynamic somatic growth feedback (\(dw/dt\)).

The mizer Reformulation (Hartvig et al. 2011; Scott et al. 2014)

Thirty-four years after (Andersen and Ursin 1977), (Hartvig et al. 2011) restored the full bioenergetic growth and reproduction equations of Andersen & Ursin within a continuous McKendrick–von Foerster PDE framework, replacing the arbitrary species-specific half-saturation constants \(Q_j(w_j)\) with a mechanistic volumetric search rate \(\gamma_j(w_j) = \gamma_j w_j^q\).

In mizer, the encounter rate of food is \(E_j(w_j) = \gamma_j(w_j) \Phi_j(w_j)\), which implies an emergent half-saturation food density \(Q_j(w_j) = \frac{h_j w_j^n}{\gamma_j w_j^q}\). All life-history parameters scale allometrically with individual weight \(w\) and asymptotic species size \(W_{\infty, j}\), reducing the parameter space from hundreds of cohort-specific constants to a compact set of scaling exponents and species traits.

1.4 Community-Level Dynamics and the Multispecies Jacobian Matrix

When comparing multispecies stock assessment models across these structural paradigms (Tsou and Collie 2001; Aydin et al. 2005; Jacobsen et al. 2016; Spence et al. 2018; Pope et al. 2019), (Pope et al. 2006), (Pope et al. 2019), and (Jacobsen et al. 2016) introduced two unifying analytical diagnostics:

1.4.1 The Yield Vector and Jacobian Interaction Matrix (\(\mathbf{J}\))

Let \(\mathbf{F} = (F_1, F_2, \dots, F_S)^\top\) be the vector of long-term fishing mortality rates across \(S\) species, and let \(\mathbf{Y}(\mathbf{F}) = (Y_1, Y_2, \dots, Y_S)^\top\) and \(\mathbf{SSB}(\mathbf{F}) = (\text{SSB}_1, \dots, \text{SSB}_S)^\top\) be the resulting equilibrium yield and spawning stock biomass vectors. Near a reference fishing vector \(\mathbf{F}_0\), the response of the multispecies community is characterized by the Jacobian matrix (Pope et al. 2019, 5):

\[J_{ik} = \left.\frac{\partial \ln \text{SSB}_i}{\partial F_k}\right|_{\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{F}_0} \quad \text{or} \quad J^{(Y)}_{ik} = \left.\frac{\partial Y_i}{\partial F_k}\right|_{\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{F}_0} \tag{1.18}\]

  • Diagonal elements (\(J_{ii} < 0\)): Measure the direct single-species depletion + intraspecific cannibalism recovery response of species \(i\) to its own fishing mortality \(F_i\). Strong cannibalism (e.g., cod, whiting, hake) dampens \(|J_{ii}|\) because fishing down large adults releases juveniles from cannibalistic \(M2\).
  • Off-diagonal elements (\(J_{ik}\) for \(i \neq k\)):
    • In one-way models (SMS, LeMans), \(J_{\text{prey}, \text{pred}} > 0\) (fishing down a predator increases prey SSB by reducing \(M2\)), while \(J_{\text{pred}, \text{prey}} \approx 0\) (fishing down a prey has zero direct effect on predator SSB, or only an indirect “prey-switching/shielding” effect if total suitable food \(\Phi_{\text{pred}}\) changes cannibalism rates).
    • In two-way bioenergetic models (mizer, Gadget, Ecosim), \(J_{\text{pred}, \text{prey}} < 0\) because fishing down forage fish reduces predator feeding level \(f_{\text{pred}}\), slowing predator somatic growth \(g_{\text{pred}}\) and lowering reproductive output \(R_{p,\text{pred}}\).

1.4.2 Quadratic Approximation of Total Multispecies Yield

(Pope et al. 2006) and (Pope et al. 2019) showed that across widely different multispecies models of the North Sea (SMS, Gadget, EwE, LeMans, mizer, CSMS, CSM), the equilibrium yield of species \(i\) as a function of the fishing mortality vector \(\mathbf{F}\) is well approximated by a coupled quadratic form (a multispecies generalization of the Schaefer surplus production parabola):

\[Y_i(\mathbf{F}) \approx a_i F_i + \sum_{k=1}^{S} b_{ik} F_i F_k \tag{1.19}\]

In matrix notation, total community yield \(Y_{\text{tot}}(\mathbf{F}) = \sum_{i=1}^S Y_i(\mathbf{F}) = \mathbf{a}^\top \mathbf{F} + \mathbf{F}^\top \mathbf{B} \mathbf{F}\). An unconstrained community-wide MSY exists at \(\mathbf{F}_{\text{MSY}} = -\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{B}^\top)^{-1}\mathbf{a}\) only if the symmetric part of the interaction matrix \(\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{B} + \mathbf{B}^\top)\) is negative definite; in practice, strong predation terms \(b_{ik} > 0\) often make the unconstrained optimum corner-valued (“fish out the predators to maximize forage fish yield”), necessitating conservation constraints (\(B_i \ge B_{\text{lim}, i}\)) or value-weighted objective functions (Pope et al. 2019).
